# Build the emergency fund before buying equities

Before putting money into stocks, a household should hold enough cash to cover
three to six months of essential expenses. This buffer is what stops a job loss
or a medical bill from forcing you to sell investments at the worst possible
time. Equity is volatile in the short run, so money you might need within a year
does not belong in it. A useful sequence is: clear high-interest debt, fund the
emergency reserve, then invest the surplus. In India this reserve is usually
parked in a sweep-in fixed deposit or a liquid fund for easy access.

A common mistake is starting a large SIP while still carrying a credit-card
balance at 36% annual interest — the guaranteed cost of that debt is higher than
any reasonable expected equity return.

# Position sizing and the single-stock cap

Position sizing is deciding how much of your capital to put into one holding.
The goal is to survive being wrong: no single stock should be large enough that
its failure derails the whole portfolio. A widely used rule of thumb is to cap
any one stock at around 5–10% of the equity portion for a concentrated investor,
and lower for a beginner. Diversifying across sectors matters as much as across
names, because stocks in the same sector tend to fall together.

Rule of thumb: single-stock weight = min(conviction-based target, portfolio cap).

Common mistakes include averaging down aggressively into a losing position until
it becomes an oversized part of the portfolio, and treating a high-conviction
idea as a reason to ignore the cap.

# Reading the P/E ratio

The price-to-earnings ratio is the share price divided by earnings per share. It
tells you how many rupees the market is paying for one rupee of annual profit. A
high P/E means the market expects strong growth; a low P/E can mean either a
bargain or a business in trouble. P/E is only meaningful when compared to the
company's own history and to its sector — a P/E of 60 can be cheap for a fast
grower and expensive for a utility.

Formula: P/E = Price / Earnings per share (EPS).

The biggest mistakes are comparing P/E across unrelated sectors, and trusting a
low trailing P/E without checking whether earnings are about to fall. In India,
remember that reported earnings can be lumpy for cyclical businesses, so a single
year's P/E can mislead.
